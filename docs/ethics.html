Data Ethics

Author

Elene Barbakadze

Published

November 12, 2025

In 2014, Amazon began developing an automated system to assist with resume screening. The goal was to manage large applicant pools more efficiently and create a consistent evaluation process. Engineers trained the model on a decade of historical hiring data, assuming that patterns in past decisions would reveal which skills and experiences Amazon valued. The system was also expected to reduce subjectivity by standardizing how resumes were scored.

By 2018, Amazon discontinued the project. Reporting from Reuters shows that the model learned gender-correlated patterns from the historical data rather than neutral indicators of qualification (Reuters, 2018). Because the majority of past applicants were men, the algorithm interpreted male-associated terms as stronger signals of competence. It penalized resumes containing phrases like “women’s,” downgraded applicants from women’s colleges, and rewarded language more common in male-dominated fields. The model never explicitly used gender as a feature, but its reliance on proxy variables reproduced existing inequities in Amazon’s hiring history.

Anti-discrimination law prohibits employers from making decisions based on gender, even indirectly. Once the bias was detected, discontinuing the model became necessary. However, the broader trend toward automated hiring accelerated regardless. A 2024 Gallup report found that the vast majority of Fortune 500 HR leaders had already integrated some form of AI into their hiring workflows (Gallup, 2024). Concerns about transparency and fairness remain widespread. In 2024, Derek Mobley filed a discrimination lawsuit against Workday, alleging that its algorithmic screening system rejected applicants in ways that disproportionately harmed job seekers based on race, age, and disability (Forbes, 2025).

Amazon continues to incorporate AI-based tools in parts of its hiring pipeline, but the company has provided limited public detail about how these systems operate or how they are evaluated for bias. The situation highlights a clear tension: the data science component is the machine-learning model that identifies patterns in past hiring decisions, while the ethical challenge lies in the model’s tendency to reproduce existing biases and its lack of transparency to those affected by automated decisions.

From Data Values and Principles manifesto

As data teams, we aim to…

  1. Principle 1 — Use data to improve life for our users, customers, organizations, and communities.

Amazon’s statement on their AI use in hiring reads - 

“Over the last several years, we’ve built tools using AI and ML to help job applicants throughout the hiring process—from identifying the right position to apply for, to giving them more flexibility through online assessments that help us engage and evaluate candidates—all while driving equity and fairness in the hiring process and adhering to legal standards.” (Amazon Staff, 2025)

AI systems used in hiring are often promoted as tools that can streamline screening and reduce subjectivity (EEOC, 2023). However, the outcome is drastically different from the anticipated utopia. In Amazon’s case, women were penalized for using terms that linked to their gender, such as attending all-women’s colleges or leading women’s clubs. This outcome was unintended and accidental – the workers creating the model never would have expected that the system they built would discriminate on that basis. But still, the effect is clear – the model created to eliminate human bias only contributed to upholding it. This is damaging to everyone involved – women workers are unfairly rejected, and Amazon misses out on highly qualified candidates. As an organization, Amazon is committed to upholding values of fairness and inclusivity, and models like these contradict those ideals.


  1. Principle 7 — Recognize and mitigate bias in ourselves and in the data we use

The backlash from the initial reporting spread far beyond the tech world, and conversations about AI ethics quickly moved into the mainstream. While Amazon never released full details about its hiring model, reporting from Reuters makes clear that the system exhibited gender-related bias because it learned patterns from historical resume data rather than from explicitly encoded rules (Reuters, 2018). When asked about the tool, an Amazon spokesperson declined to comment on its specific failures and emphasized that the model had never been used to make final hiring decisions.

The underlying issue was not that Amazon intentionally designed a sexist model, but that the system reflected patterns in the company’s own historical hiring practices. Because the model was trained on a decade of resumes submitted to Amazon, it identified male-associated terms as stronger signals of qualification than female-associated ones. This kind of outcome is common when machine-learning systems ingest biased datasets (Bogen & Rieke, 2018). Acknowledging this publicly would have required Amazon to confront long-standing inequities in its own hiring pipeline.

Transparency and accountability are essential for building trust in automated hiring systems, yet Amazon provided limited visibility into how the model operated or why it failed. Although the company ultimately discontinued the system, it has not released meaningful details about what was learned from the incident or how similar risks will be addressed going forward.


  1. Principle 10 — Respect and invite fair criticism while promoting the identification and open discussion of errors, risks, and unintended consequences of our work.

Amazon’s handling of its hiring model reflects a clear breakdown of this principle. After internal teams discovered that the model exhibited gender-related bias, the company provided very little public explanation about what went wrong or how the issue was addressed. The system was never released for independent auditing, and researchers had no opportunity to examine the model’s behavior or identify where the bias originated. Instead of encouraging open discussion, Amazon offered minimal commentary and framed the tool as an experiment that was never fully deployed. This reluctance to engage with external critique prevented meaningful public scrutiny, limited the possibility of fixing underlying problems, and weakened trust in the company’s broader use of AI in hiring.


  1. Principle 6: Be open to changing our methods and conclusions in response to new knowledge.

With experimental and breakthrough work, some complications are inevitable. The same can be said for Amazon’s hiring model. While the system remains private and its training data unavailable to the public, Reuters reported that Amazon’s team had edited the model to reduce gender bias. Still, the underlying issue persists. The logic behind AI decision-making is often opaque, and identifying bias requires extensive testing and transparency. At present, it’s impossible to know what specific factors the model may use to discriminate against candidates. There are countless subtle ways for an AI to reproduce human bias – through zip codes, first and last names, student organizations, or even languages spoken.

Amazon acknowledges this risk, stating:

“As part of this process, we start with user research—diving deep to understand the context for our tools from diverse and representative perspectives and experiences. Prior to launching any new tool, we conduct studies ensuring that the tool is similarly benefiting individuals from diverse identity groups.”

However, despite this apparent commitment to equity, it remains unclear what concrete steps the company has taken to ensure that past mistakes are not repeated.


Summary

Amazon’s hiring model highlights how easily AI systems can inherit and amplify human bias. The company’s lack of transparency and resistance to outside scrutiny reveal how easy it is for bias to remain hidden behind claims of progress. As artificial intelligence becomes a standard part of hiring practices, the consequences of these systems grow even more significant. With recent cases like the lawsuit against Workday’s AI screening system, it becomes clear that these issues are not isolated mistakes but symptoms of a larger problem. Taking them seriously is crucial, as fairness and accountability must remain at the center of technological advancement.
